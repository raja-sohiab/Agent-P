# Evidence and Inference Rules

## 1. Purpose

This document defines how Agent-P should use business and website information when generating outbound emails.

The objective is to produce relevant emails without inventing company-specific facts.

Agent-P must distinguish between:

- Known facts
- Strong business-category inference
- Plausible inference
- Unsupported claims

---

# 2. Evidence Hierarchy

Use information according to the following hierarchy:

1. Explicit evidence
2. Strong category-level inference
3. Reasonable contextual inference
4. Unsupported company-specific assumption

Levels 1–3 may be usable depending on wording.

Level 4 must never be used.

---

# 3. Level 1 — Explicit Evidence

Information directly supported by the available source.

Examples:

Website states:

"Commercial roofing contractor serving property owners."

Safe conclusions:

- The company provides commercial roofing.
- Property owners may be part of its audience.
- Roofing projects are relevant to the company.

Explicit information can be stated directly when useful.

---

# 4. Level 2 — Strong Category-Level Inference

A situation that is strongly associated with the company's service or business category.

Examples:

Roofing contractor:

Property owners may need:
- Repairs
- Inspections
- Replacement
- Maintenance

Accounting firm:

Business owners may need:
- Accounting support
- Tax services
- Financial reporting
- Bookkeeping

Software consultancy:

Businesses may:
- Evaluate software
- Consider automation
- Need technical expertise

These are category-level business realities.

They do not mean the specific company currently has those customers or projects.

Use appropriate general language.

---

# 5. Level 3 — Reasonable Contextual Inference

A plausible situation that can reasonably be inferred but is not established.

Examples:

"Businesses evaluating automation may..."

"Property owners planning upgrades may..."

"Companies experiencing operational complexity might..."

Use cautious language such as:

- may
- might
- could
- often
- people considering
- businesses evaluating
- teams looking for
- organizations exploring

Do not turn the inference into a company-specific fact.

---

# 6. Level 4 — Unsupported Company-Specific Claims

These are prohibited.

Do not claim that the specific company:

- Is currently looking for customers
- Is currently looking for projects
- Is growing
- Recently expanded
- Recently launched a service
- Has a particular customer
- Has a specific problem
- Is struggling with sales
- Is hiring
- Is entering a new market
- Is planning a project
- Is seeking leads
- Is experiencing increased demand

unless the information is explicitly supported.

---

# 7. Website Information

When a website is available, use it to understand:

- What the company does
- What it sells
- What services it provides
- Who it serves
- What industries it serves
- What problems it addresses
- What type of customer may require its service

Website information should improve the reasoning.

Do not simply repeat website copy.

---

# 8. Website Detail vs Commercial Relevance

Not every website fact belongs in the email.

For example:

Website says:

"Founded in 1998 and serving three generations of customers."

This does not necessarily create a useful outbound angle.

Do not include it simply because it is available.

Prefer information that helps answer:

"Why might someone need this company's service?"

---

# 9. Customer Audience Inference

It is acceptable to infer a logical audience from the company's service.

Examples:

Roofing company
→ property owners

Commercial software provider
→ businesses

Accounting firm
→ business owners

Dental practice
→ people seeking dental care

Commercial real estate firm
→ property owners, investors, buyers, sellers, or tenants depending on the service

However, choose the audience that best fits the actual business.

---

# 10. Do Not Assume the Decision-Maker

Do not automatically assume:

- CEO
- Founder
- Owner
- CFO
- VP
- Procurement
- Marketing Director

is the correct buyer.

Determine the likely audience based on the company's actual service.

If the available information does not establish a specific decision-maker, use a broader business audience.

---

# 11. Customer Situation Inference

A plausible customer situation can be inferred when it is strongly connected to the service.

Examples:

HVAC company:
→ seasonal maintenance
→ repairs
→ replacement

Construction company:
→ new project
→ renovation
→ expansion

Software company:
→ evaluating software
→ automation
→ technical improvement

Financial advisor:
→ financial planning
→ investment decisions
→ changing financial needs

The situation must remain plausible.

---

# 12. Do Not Assume Current Intent

A general customer situation does not prove current intent.

Avoid:

"Your customers are currently looking for..."

Prefer:

"Customers considering..."

"Businesses evaluating..."

"Property owners planning..."

"People looking for..."

---

# 13. Company-Specific vs Category-Level Statements

### Company-specific

"Your company is currently expanding into commercial construction."

Requires explicit evidence.

### Category-level

"Commercial construction companies often work with property owners planning new projects."

Generally acceptable as business reasoning.

Agent-P must keep these levels separate.

---

# 14. Claims About Demand

Do not claim that there is currently high demand unless supported.

Avoid:

"There's huge demand for your services."

"Businesses are actively searching for your service."

"Your market is booming."

Prefer:

"Businesses considering..."

"Companies evaluating..."

"Customers who may be looking for..."

---

# 15. Claims About Competitors

Do not introduce competitor information unless specifically supported and necessary.

Do not claim:

- The company is losing business to competitors
- Competitors are outperforming them
- Customers prefer competitors
- The company needs to differentiate

unless explicitly supported.

---

# 16. Claims About Growth

Do not infer company growth from:

- Website quality
- Number of services
- Social media activity
- Company age
- Number of employees
- General industry growth

Use growth-related reasoning only at the general category level.

Example:

"Growing businesses often need..."

not:

"Your growing business needs..."

unless supported.

---

# 17. Claims About Problems

Do not assume the company has a specific problem.

Avoid:

"You're probably struggling to generate leads."

"Your sales team may be having trouble..."

unless the input provides evidence.

Instead, focus on customer situations and potential commercial opportunities.

---

# 18. Product Claims

PersistIQ value must also follow evidence rules.

Do not guarantee:

- Leads
- Meetings
- Customers
- Revenue
- Replies
- Sales
- Conversions
- Pipeline
- Results

Use:

"could help"

or equivalent cautious positioning.

---

# 19. Business-Type Knowledge

General business knowledge can be used to reason about:

- Customers
- Services
- Common needs
- Commercial situations
- Buying triggers

General business knowledge must not be converted into unsupported company-specific facts.

---

# 20. Multiple Possible Interpretations

If the available information supports several possible business interpretations:

1. Prefer the interpretation directly supported by evidence.
2. Prefer the interpretation requiring the fewest assumptions.
3. Prefer the interpretation that creates the clearest service connection.
4. Use cautious language when necessary.

Do not select the most creative interpretation simply because it creates a more interesting email.

---

# 21. Missing Information

Missing information does not justify invention.

If the website does not establish a specific audience or situation:

Use the strongest reasonable category-level inference.

Keep the language broad enough to remain accurate.

---

# 22. Conflicting Information

If different sources provide conflicting information:

1. Prefer the most direct and current business information available.
2. Avoid relying on uncertain claims.
3. Use broader language when necessary.
4. Do not combine conflicting information into a new unsupported conclusion.

---

# 23. Relevance Over Specificity

More specific does not automatically mean better.

The objective is:

Accurate relevance
+
Commercial usefulness
+
Natural language

not maximum detail.

A concise, accurate inference is better than a highly specific unsupported claim.

---

# 24. Final Evidence Test

Before approving an email, ask:

"Is every company-specific statement supported?"

Then ask:

"Are any inferred situations clearly presented as possibilities rather than facts?"

If a claim cannot pass either test, rewrite it.

---

# 25. Core Principle

Use evidence to become more relevant.

Do not use assumptions to appear more personalized.

Accurate reasoning
>
fabricated personalization.
